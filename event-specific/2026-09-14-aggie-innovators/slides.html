The cost of starting over

AI can lose the context
that makes your work yours.

A new conversation may not know your team’s decisions, your definition of “done,” or why you rejected an earlier idea.

You repeat the background.

Who the work serves, what matters, and what has already been tried.

The same mistakes return.

A discarded idea comes back. An old assumption becomes a new answer.

The next person starts cold.

The result survives, but the reasoning and unfinished work stay in your chat.

For example: your team decided to exclude pending sales from the weekly revenue report. A new chat includes them again because it never received that decision.
What the theme means

Keep your way of working.
Change the tools when you need to.

Your operating model is how work gets done: what you are trying to achieve, who decides, how you check quality, and what you learn.

What you should own

Requirements, procedures, decisions, sources, and the record of what happened.

Keep them in formats your people and tools can read.

What you can replace

The model, chat app, coding assistant, or task-management tool.

A better tool should not require rebuilding your organization’s knowledge.

For example: an expense report still needs receipts and a manager’s approval when you switch accounting software. Your rules belong to your business; the software helps carry them out.
AI foundations / model

A model is the trained system
that generates an answer.

An AI model learns patterns from training data. When you give it input, it uses those learned patterns to generate an output. A language model generates text one piece at a time.

Your inputQuestion, instructions, documents, images
→
Trained modelUses learned patterns and the supplied context
→
Generated outputAn explanation, draft, code, or proposed tool action

Training is its background.

Broad learned patterns are not the same as knowing your current project.

Context is what it sees now.

Your prompt, conversation, and supplied or retrieved information.

An answer needs checking.

A fluent response can still contain incorrect facts or unsupported assumptions.

AI foundations / agent

An agent uses AI to take actions
toward a goal.

An AI agent uses a model to decide what to do next, uses tools to do it, and responds to the results. It can carry out several steps instead of stopping at an answer.

GoalPrepare a weekly sales report
→
ActionRead orders and calculate revenue
→
FeedbackTwo orders are still pending
→
Next stepExclude them and explain why
The difference: a chatbot can explain how to prepare a report. An agent can read the source files, build it, check it, and save the result. It still needs limits and a human owner.
AI foundations / harness

A harness is the software
that lets a model do useful work.

The harness connects the model to its environment. It supplies context, makes tools available, controls what actions are allowed, and returns results so the model can continue.

Harness
Files + instructionsTools + integrationsModelPermissions + limitsResults + work state
For example: Codex gives a model access to project files and development tools. The model proposes an action; the harness executes it within the configured permissions.
Our demo / student entrepreneurship

Aggie Launchpad:
from an idea to a useful experiment.

Jordan wants to start Campus Cycle: a bicycle-repair pickup service for students. Before building the business, Jordan needs to learn whether students will actually use it.

The app we will build

A founder dashboard connecting assumptions, customer evidence, a pilot experiment, and next decisions.

What we know so far

20 interviews. 12 students expressed interest. Six reserved a free pilot slot. Nobody has paid.

The decision ahead

Run a small pilot if at least five reservations remain active. Jordan decides whether to proceed.

The challenge: two students cancel. Does the app still say “ready to launch,” or help the founder understand what changed? All people and research data are fictional.
Aggie Launchpad / starting from a brief

An attractive startup dashboard
can still tell the wrong story.

“Build an app to help me validate my startup idea” is easy to ask. The useful result depends on what the app treats as evidence.

Interest ≠ a reservation

A positive interview response does not mean a student booked a pilot slot.

A reservation ≠ a sale

A free pilot can test participation. It cannot prove willingness to pay.

A target ≠ approval

Meeting five reservations allows a founder review. It does not authorize a launch.

A useful dashboard makes uncertainty visible instead of turning every positive signal into “validated.”
What useful output should answer

Can the founder make
a better decision?

What do we know?

Show interview interest and active reservations separately, with their sources.

What are we testing?

At least five active reservations for a free pickup pilot. Paid demand remains untested.

What happens next?

Recalculate after cancellations, flag a missed target, and name the decision owner.

The test of the app: can another teammate explain the evidence and next decision without reading Jordan’s entire chat history?

Putting the framework into practice

Give the next experiment
something useful to inherit.

Requirements

Keep interview interest, reservations, and paid demand separate. Show sources and uncertainty.

Procedure

Recount when evidence changes. Compare with the target. Route the next decision to the founder.

Memory

Retain the hypothesis, target, source records, decision history, and unanswered questions.

A reusable rule: “Never turn an expression of interest into a paying customer.” That correction should survive the next project and the next tool.

A practical first step

Start with one decision
you want AI to help you make.

Choose a customer experiment, research summary, weekly report, or project handoff. Write a short working agreement before asking AI to help.

What counts as evidence?

“Separate customer behavior from what people say they might do.”

Who decides?

“The founder accepts the next experiment. AI proposes and checks.”

What should survive?

“Save the sources, decision, reasoning, and next question.”

Your first move: write those three answers for your own task, try them with your current tool, and retain the corrections.
