# Speaker Notes: 20-Minute Version

## 1. Build in public. Trust yourself to figure it out.

0:00–0:30. I want to leave you with two ideas that have become more important as AI has become more capable: let people see the useful things you are learning and building, and have faith in your ability to figure out the next problem. Those ideas belong together. Public work gives people evidence of how you think. Solving unfamiliar problems gives you something useful to share.

## 2. What public work made possible

0:30–3:00. I wish I had begun building in public much earlier. I did plenty of work, but I left too little public trail showing people how I approached a problem. When I finally started sharing my work, that trail created opportunities I never could have planned. It led to the opportunity to join the OpenClaw team. That led to hosting an event and recording a video and podcast with GitHub. It also led to about a dozen speaking engagements and appearances. People could see how I thought, what I made, how I contributed, and how I handled problems before we ever spoke. Those opportunities happened because I built in public. That is why I wish I had started sooner.

## 3. Personal brand is accumulated evidence

3:00–4:30. AI can help almost anyone draft, research, code, design, and learn. That makes human trust more valuable. People still want to know who understands their problem, who follows through, and who will tell the truth when an answer is not ready. Your personal brand is the reputation created by your actions and the evidence you leave behind. A logo may represent it. Your actions define what people expect from you before you enter the room.

## 4. Share the failures too

4:30–6:30. Building in public lets people watch you learn. Share what you tried, what happened, and what you learned. Explain something you just learned. Show a project while it is taking shape. Share a failure and what you changed. Answer a real question from your field. You often learn more from the attempt that missed than the one that worked the first time. People empathize because they see their own setbacks in yours. Put the lesson ahead of the spectacle. Show the correction too. A failure followed by a better attempt becomes proof of how you respond when work gets hard. Protect what is private or belongs to someone else.

## 5. Community changes the work

6:30–7:45. Get involved locally. Help someone without keeping score. Teach a class, answer a question, volunteer, mentor, or show up when a community needs hands. Giving back makes the community stronger, but it also changes you. You learn how different people see the same problem. You practice explaining ideas in a way that makes sense outside your own bubble. Those relationships outlast any single platform.

## 6. Three books. Three practices.

7:45–9:45. Three books helped shape how I think about the rest of this talk. Meditations is a collection of Marcus Aurelius's private reflections. He was leading the Roman Empire and still returned to examining his choices. The lesson I took from it is that consistency drives success. Nuts! is by Kevin and Jackie Freiberg. It tells the story of Herb Kelleher and Southwest Airlines. I took a simple question from Kelleher's example: Why not? Little teams topple behemoths all the time. Why not you? Simon Sinek's Leaders Eat Last reinforced that true leadership is serving. Leadership shows up in what you make possible for other people, especially when there is no immediate reward for you.

## 7. Confidence needs a process

9:45–11:45. Faith in your ability to figure things out rests on a process. Confidence grows as you practice it. When someone gives me a problem, I first try to understand it and make progress myself. AI gives me access to explanations, examples, research, and working tools. I can learn the language of a new domain faster than I could before. Then I check the answer. I compare sources, test what can be tested, and take the result back to the people who know the work. How you approach a problem is often more valuable than what you knew before the problem arrived. For consequential decisions, AI helps you prepare better questions. Qualified people still own the decision.

## 8. Empathy before implementation

11:45–13:45. Customers rarely arrive with a perfectly defined problem. They describe a symptom: work takes too long, information gets lost, customers keep calling, or the team does not trust the numbers. I sometimes describe our approach as McKinsey & Company for geeks. We bring consulting curiosity, then stay close enough to build the working solution. The first tool is a question: Show me how the work happens today. Then listen to the people who do it. Ask where the work becomes frustrating, what exceptions keep happening, and what a better day would look like. Empathy keeps us from solving the impressive problem instead of the real one.

## 9. I know code. I don’t know dough.

13:45–16:00. Here is a fictional challenge: I know code. I do not know dough. A bakery sells out of customer favorites while throwing away other bread at closing. The owner wants help deciding what to bake tomorrow. I do not need to pretend I am a baker. I use AI to learn the vocabulary and prepare better questions, then ask the baker to show me how they plan a day. Sales records alone may hide demand: once an item sells out, the till cannot record people who wanted it. I ask about sellout times, advance orders, batch sizes, and what can realistically be changed before tomorrow. Then I build a small forecast using the available history and confirmed orders. AI can help me explore the data and build the tool; the baker reviews quantities and practical constraints. We test one product family, compare the recommendations with the bakery’s usual plan, and track both unsold items and sellouts. The outcome is unknown until we test. My value is a method for learning and solving the problem with the expert, not arriving with an expert’s knowledge of bread.

## 10. What deserves your focus

16:00–17:30. There will always be more things that matter than you can personally control. There will also be things you can control that have little impact. Put your energy in the overlap. You can control whether you keep learning, whether you publish something useful, whether you help your community, and whether you make the next responsible attempt.

## 11. How badly do you want it?

17:30–18:30. Eric Thomas, the Hip Hop Preacher, says: When you want to succeed as bad as you want to breathe, then you'll be successful. That desire is fuel. Your process gives it direction. Wanting the result matters, but you still have to learn, try, listen, correct, and keep going.

Sources:

## 12. One useful thing this week

18:30–20:00. Publish one useful thing this week. Share something you learned, show a small thing you made, explain how you helped solve a problem, or show a correction after a failure. Put it where people in your field can find it. Protect work that belongs to someone else. You can begin before you know everything. Bring enough curiosity to take the next step and enough humility to check what you got wrong. If I can do this, you can do this. Start small, think big.
